\section{Local Volatility Model}

We are going to introduce the Local Volatility Model mainly following \cite{bergomi_stochastic_vol}.

\subsection{Model description}

The \textbf{local volatility model} is the simplest and most widely used volatility model in derivatives pricing.
It extends the Black-Scholes framework by allowing the instantaneous volatility to be a deterministic function of both time and the spot price of the underlying asset,
\begin{equation*}
    \sigma = \sigma(t, S).
\end{equation*}
So the dynamics of the underlying asset under the local volatility model are given by the following stochastic differential equation,
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{dS_t}{S_t} = (r - q)\, dt + \sigma(t, S_t)\, dW_t,
\end{equation*}
and the pricing PDE is identical to the Black-Scholes PDE,
with the deterministic volatility function replacing the constant volatility
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} =
        r f(t, S_t)
        - (r - q) S_t \frac{\partial f}{\partial S}
        - \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2(t, S_t) S_t^2 \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial S^2},
\end{equation*}

\subsubsection{Market model}

A market model is a valuation model that includes vanilla options as part of the underlying,
this means we are assuming that when hedging a derivative,
we can use the vanilla options as a hedging instrument,
in other words,
the derivative can be replicated using a combination of the underlying as well as vanilla options.
Thus vanilla options under a market model are exactly priced.

The local volatility model is a market model,
since it is calibrated to match the vanilla option prices observed in the market

\subsection{Dupire Equation}

The Dupire equation, introduced in \cite{dupire_pricing_smile},
expresses the local volatility function $\sigma(t, S)$ directly in terms of derivatives
of call option prices with respect to strike and maturity.
We present the derivation following \cite{bergomi_stochastic_vol}.

\subsubsection{Derivation}

Consider the following general diffusive dynamics for $S_t$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:log-normal_diffusion}
    dS_t = (r - q)\,S_t\,dt + \sigma_t\,S_t\,dW_t,
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_t$ is an arbitrary adapted process.
The price of a European call option with strike $K$ and maturity $T$ under the risk-neutral measure is:
\begin{equation*}
    C(K, T) = e^{-rT}\,\E\!\left[(S_T - K)^+\right].
\end{equation*}

Before applying It\^{o}'s formula we recall the two generalized functions
that appear as distributional derivatives of $(x)^+ = \max(x, 0)$.

\begin{definition}[Heaviside function]
The \textbf{Heaviside function} $\theta : \R \to \{0,1\}$ is defined by
\begin{equation*}
    \theta(x) =
    \begin{cases}
        1 & x > 0, \\
        0 & x < 0.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation*}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Dirac delta]
The \textbf{Dirac delta} $\delta$ is the distributional derivative of $\theta$,
characterized by the property that for any smooth test function $\varphi$:
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} \varphi(x)\,\delta(x)\,dx = \varphi(0).
\end{equation*}
\end{definition}

These two distributions arise as the successive generalized derivatives of $(x)^+$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:heaviside_delta_max}
    \frac{d}{dx}(x)^+ = \theta(x),
    \qquad
    \frac{d^2}{dx^2}(x)^+ = \delta(x).
\end{equation}
The first identity follows from $(x)^+ = x\,\theta(x)$ and $x\,\delta(x) = 0$;
the second is the distributional derivative of $\theta$.

To relate option prices to the dynamics of $S_t$, we apply It\^{o}'s formula
to $(S_T - K)^+$ over the interval $[T, T + dT]$, using \eqref{eq:heaviside_delta_max}
with $x = S_T - K$:
\begin{align*}
    d(S_T - K)^+
    &= \theta(S_T - K)\bigl((r-q)\,S_T\,dT + \sigma_T\,S_T\,dW_T\bigr) \\
    &\quad + \frac{1}{2}\,\delta(S_T - K)\,\sigma_T^2\,S_T^2\,dT.
\end{align*}
We work with undiscounted prices $\mathcal{C}(K,T) = e^{rT} C(K,T)$.
Differentiating the identity $\mathcal{C}(K,T) = \E[(S_T - K)^+]$ with respect to $K$ gives:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dupire_density}
    \E\!\left[\theta(S_T - K)\right] = -\frac{\partial \mathcal{C}}{\partial K},
    \qquad
    \E\!\left[\delta(S_T - K)\right] = \frac{\partial^2 \mathcal{C}}{\partial K^2}.
\end{equation}
The second identity is the Breeden--Litzenberger result \cite{breeden_litzenberger}: the second strike derivative
of undiscounted call prices equals the risk-neutral density of $S_T$.
To see why, let $p(\cdot, T)$ denote the risk-neutral density of $S_T$, so that
\begin{equation*}
    \mathcal{C}(K, T)
        = \E[(S_T - K)^+]
        = \int_0^{\infty} (s - K)^+\, p(s, T)\,ds
        = \int_K^{\infty} (s - K)\, p(s, T)\,ds.
\end{equation*}
Differentiating with respect to $K$ (using Leibniz's rule, noting the lower limit contributes zero
because $(K-K)^+ = 0$):
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\partial \mathcal{C}}{\partial K}
        = -\int_K^{\infty} p(s, T)\,ds
        = -(1 - F(K, T)),
\end{equation*}
where $F(K, T) = P(S_T \leq K)$ is the risk-neutral CDF.
A second differentiation gives:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:breeden_litzenberger}
    \frac{\partial^2 \mathcal{C}}{\partial K^2} = p(K, T).
\end{equation}
Since $\E[\delta(S_T - K)] = \int_0^\infty \delta(s - K)\,p(s,T)\,ds = p(K,T)$,
this is exactly the second identity in~\eqref{eq:dupire_density}.
In particular, the positivity of $p$ guarantees $\partial^2 \mathcal{C}/\partial K^2 \geq 0$,
which we will use when discussing no-arbitrage conditions.

From the identity $\mathcal{C} = \E[S_T\,\theta(S_T - K)] - K\,\E[\theta(S_T - K)]$ we also obtain:
\begin{equation*}
    \E\!\left[S_T\,\theta(S_T - K)\right] = \mathcal{C} - K\frac{\partial \mathcal{C}}{\partial K}.
\end{equation*}

Taking the expectation of the It\^{o} expansion (noting $\E[dW_T] = 0$) and substituting these identities:
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
    \frac{\partial \mathcal{C}}{\partial T}\,dT
        & = (r-q)\,\E[S_T\,\theta(S_T-K)]\,dT
        + \frac{1}{2}\,\E\!\left[\sigma_T^2\,S_T^2\,\delta(S_T - K)\right]dT \\
        & = (r-q)\!\left(\mathcal{C} - K\frac{\partial \mathcal{C}}{\partial K}\right)dT
        + \frac{1}{2}\,\E\!\left[\sigma_T^2\,S_T^2\,\delta(S_T - K)\right]dT.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
For the remaining term, we use the sifting property of the Dirac delta:
since $\delta(S_T - K)$ is supported at $S_T = K$, the factor $S_T^2$ can be replaced by $K^2$ inside any expectation,
\begin{equation*}
    \E\!\left[\sigma_T^2\,S_T^2\,\delta(S_T - K)\right] = K^2\,\E\!\left[\sigma_T^2\,\delta(S_T - K)\right],
\end{equation*}
giving:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\partial \mathcal{C}}{\partial T}\,dT
    = (r-q)\!\left(\mathcal{C} - K\frac{\partial \mathcal{C}}{\partial K}\right)dT
    + \frac{K^2}{2}\,\E\!\left[\sigma_T^2\,\delta(S_T - K)\right]dT.
\end{equation*}
Solving for $\E[\sigma_T^2\,\delta(S_T - K)]$, dividing by
$\E[\delta(S_T - K)] = \partial^2\mathcal{C}/\partial K^2$,
and reverting to discounted prices $C = e^{-rT}\mathcal{C}$, we obtain:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dupire_general}
    \E\!\left[\sigma_T^2 \,\middle|\, S_T = K\right]
    = 2\,\frac{\dfrac{\partial C}{\partial T} + qC + (r-q)\,K\,\dfrac{\partial C}{\partial K}}
           {K^2\,\dfrac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}}.
\end{equation}
This identity holds for any model of the lognormal-type form \ref{eq:log-normal_diffusion},
with $\sigma_t$ an arbitrary adapted process \cite{bergomi_stochastic_vol}.
The multiplicative structure $\sigma_t S_t$ is essential: it is what produces the factor $S_T^2$ in the It\^{o} expansion,
which the sifting property then converts to $K^2$.
A consequence is that the smile alone does not determine $\sigma_t$:
two processes $\sigma_t$ and $\sigma_t'$ generate the same vanilla smile if and only if
$\E[\sigma_T^2 \mid S_T = K] = \E[\sigma_T^{\prime 2} \mid S_T = K]$ for all $K$ and $T$.

In the local volatility model, where $\sigma_t \equiv \sigma(t, S_t)$,
the conditional expectation reduces to $\sigma(t,S)^2$,
and evaluating at $K = S$, $T = t$ gives the \textbf{Dupire formula}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dupire_formula}
    \sigma(t, S)^2 = 2\,\frac{
        \dfrac{\partial C}{\partial T} + qC + (r-q)\,K\,\dfrac{\partial C}{\partial K}
    }{
        K^2\,\dfrac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}
    }\Bigg|_{\substack{K = S,\\ T = t}}.
\end{equation}
Given the full surface of observed call prices $C(K,T)$,
equation~\eqref{eq:dupire_formula} uniquely determines the local volatility function.

\subsubsection{Forward equation}

Rearranging \eqref{eq:dupire_general} for a known local volatility function $\sigma(t, S)$
yields the \textbf{Dupire forward equation}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dupire_forward}
    \frac{\partial C}{\partial T} + (r-q)\,K\,\frac{\partial C}{\partial K}
    - \frac{\sigma^2(T,K)}{2}\,K^2\,\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2} = -qC.
\end{equation}
Unlike the Black-Scholes backward PDE, which is solved backwards in $t$
from a terminal payoff for a single strike and maturity,
the forward equation propagates forward in $T$ with initial condition
$C(K, T = 0) = (S_0 - K)^+$,
producing prices for a fixed initial spot $S_0$ but all strikes $K$ and maturities $T$ simultaneously.

\subsubsection{No-arbitrage conditions}

For the Dupire formula~\eqref{eq:dupire_formula} to yield a positive local variance,
both the numerator and denominator of~\eqref{eq:dupire_general} must be strictly positive,
which is equivalent to requiring the option surface to be free of arbitrage.

\paragraph{Strike arbitrage.}
From~\eqref{eq:dupire_density}, the denominator satisfies:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\partial^2 C(K,T)}{\partial K^2} = e^{-rT}\,\E\!\left[\delta(S_T - K)\right] \geq 0,
\end{equation*}
since it is proportional to the risk-neutral density of $S_T$.
A violation can be exploited via a \textit{butterfly spread}:
buying $\varepsilon^{-2}$ calls at strike $K-\varepsilon$,
buying $\varepsilon^{-2}$ calls at strike $K+\varepsilon$,
and selling $2\varepsilon^{-2}$ calls at strike $K$.
This portfolio has a non-negative payoff at maturity for any realisation of $S_T$,
so its inception value must be non-negative, implying $\partial^2 C/\partial K^2 \geq 0$.

\paragraph{Maturity arbitrage.}
The numerator of~\eqref{eq:dupire_general} can be rewritten as:
\begin{equation*}
    e^{-qT}\frac{d}{dT}\!\left[e^{qT}\,C\!\left(K e^{(r-q)T}, T\right)\right],
\end{equation*}
which is non-negative if and only if $e^{qT}$ times the price of a call struck at
a fixed proportion of the forward $F_T = S_0 e^{(r-q)T}$ is non-decreasing in maturity.
A violation constitutes a \textit{maturity arbitrage}:
one can buy a longer-dated option and sell a shorter-dated one at a net positive premium,
with a delta position that generates non-negative P\&L at both maturities.

In an arbitrage-free market both conditions hold and
the Dupire local volatility function is well-defined.

\subsection{Calibration problem}

The local volatility model must be calibrated to market option prices to be usable.
Given quoted call prices $C^*(K_i, T_j)$ for a finite set of strikes and maturities,
we seek a function $\sigma(S,t)$, or equivalently $a(y,\tau) = \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2$,
such that the Dupire forward equation reproduces the observed prices.
This is an ill-posed inverse problem: market data are noisy, sparse in maturity,
and the map from $a$ to option prices has a smoothing effect that makes its inverse discontinuous.
We follow \cite{egger2005tikhonov} and treat the calibration as a Tikhonov regularization problem.

\subsection{Tikhonov regularization of the inverse pricing problem}

\subsubsection{Change of variable --- log-moneyness}

Market option prices depend on the ratio $K/S_0$ rather than on the absolute strike $K$.
Working in log-moneyness
\begin{equation*}
    y = \log(K/S_0), \qquad \text{equivalently} \quad K = S_0\,e^{y},
\end{equation*}
removes this dependence and puts the PDE in a translation-invariant form,
just as the substitution $x = \log(S/K)$ reduced the Black--Scholes PDE to the heat
equation in Section~\ref{sec:bs_pricing}.
Define the time to maturity $\tau = T$,
the diffusion coefficient $a(y,\tau) = \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2(K,T)$,
and the normalized call price $u(y,\tau) = C(S_0; K, T)/S_0$,
so that $C(S_0; K, T) = S_0\,u(y,\tau)$.%
\footnote{We keep the explicit zero-order term. \cite{egger2005tikhonov} instead set
$u = e^{\int_t^T q\,ds}\,C$, which absorbs the dividend factor and yields the
equivalent equation $-u_\tau + a(u_{yy}-u_y) + (q-r)u_y = 0$ without a $-q\,u$ term;
the two normalizations coincide when $q = 0$, as in Example~1 below.}

We now carry the Dupire forward equation~\eqref{eq:dupire_forward} into the
$(y,\tau)$ coordinates. Since $y = \log(K/S_0)$ we have $\partial y/\partial K = 1/K$,
and because $\tau = T$ the maturity derivative is unchanged.
Applying the chain rule to $C = S_0\,u(y,\tau)$ gives the three derivatives that appear
in~\eqref{eq:dupire_forward}:
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
    \frac{\partial C}{\partial T}
        &= S_0\,u_\tau, \\
    \frac{\partial C}{\partial K}
        &= S_0\,u_y\,\frac{\partial y}{\partial K}
         = \frac{S_0}{K}\,u_y, \\
    \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}
        &= \frac{\partial}{\partial K}\!\left(\frac{S_0}{K}\,u_y\right)
         = \frac{S_0}{K}\,u_{yy}\,\frac{\partial y}{\partial K} - \frac{S_0}{K^2}\,u_y
         = \frac{S_0}{K^2}\,(u_{yy} - u_y).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
The convenient feature of log-moneyness is that the first-order operator $K\,\partial_K$
and the second-order operator $K^2\,\partial_{KK}$ both become constant-coefficient
operators in $y$:
\begin{equation*}
    K\,\frac{\partial C}{\partial K} = S_0\,u_y,
    \qquad
    K^2\,\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2} = S_0\,(u_{yy} - u_y),
\end{equation*}
which is exactly what makes the equation translation-invariant in $y$.
Substituting these into~\eqref{eq:dupire_forward} and using $a = \tfrac12\sigma^2$,
\begin{equation*}
    S_0\,u_\tau + (r-q)\,S_0\,u_y - a\,S_0\,(u_{yy} - u_y) = -q\,S_0\,u.
\end{equation*}
Dividing through by $S_0$ and solving for $u_\tau$ yields the forward equation in
log-moneyness form,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inverse_pde_1}
    u_\tau = a(y,\tau)\,(u_{yy} - u_y) - (r-q)\,u_y - q\,u,
\end{equation}
on the truncated domain $Q_{M,T} = (-M, M) \times (0, T]$.
The initial condition follows from the same normalization applied to the
call payoff $C(K,0) = (S_0 - K)^+$:
\begin{equation*}
    u(y,0) = \frac{(S_0 - K)^+}{S_0} = \left(1 - \frac{K}{S_0}\right)^{\!+} = (1 - e^{y})^+,
\end{equation*}
that is,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inverse_pde_3}
    u(y, 0) = \max(1 - e^y, 0)
\end{equation}
and Dirichlet boundary conditions
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inverse_pde_2}
    u(-M, \tau) = \Psi_1(\tau), \qquad u(M, \tau) = \Psi_2(\tau).
\end{equation}
The boundary values are determined from the Black--Scholes formula evaluated
at the boundary strikes $K = S_0 e^{\pm M}$:
as $M \to \infty$, the left boundary ($K \to 0$) gives the intrinsic value
$\Psi_1(\tau) \to e^{-q\tau}$, and the right boundary ($K \to \infty$) gives $\Psi_2(\tau) \to 0$.

\subsubsection{Parameter-to-solution map}

For each admissible diffusion coefficient $a$, let $u(a)$ denote the solution
to~\eqref{eq:inverse_pde_1}--\eqref{eq:inverse_pde_3}.
Fix an \emph{a~priori} reference $a^* \in L^\infty(\mathbb{R})$ with $0 < \underline{a} \le a^* \le \bar{a}$
and define the set of admissible parameters
\begin{equation}\label{eq:admissible_set}
    \mathcal{K}(a^*) = \bigl\{ a \in a^* + H^1(Q) : \underline{a} \le a \le \bar{a} \bigr\}.
\end{equation}
The \emph{parameter-to-solution map} $F = F_{a_0}$ is defined as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:param_solution_map}
    F(a) = u(a) - u(a_0),
\end{equation}
where $a_0 \in \mathbb{R}$ with $\underline{a} \le a_0 \le \bar{a}$ is a reference constant
and $u(a_0)$ can be computed from the Black--Scholes formula.
A key regularity result established in \cite{egger2005tikhonov} is the following.

\begin{theorem}[\cite{egger2005tikhonov}, Theorem 2.1]
There exists $p^* > 2$ such that $F\colon \mathcal{K}(a^*) \to W_p^{2,1}(Q)$
is continuous and compact for $2 \le p < p^*$, and weakly sequentially continuous.
\end{theorem}

The compactness of $F$ is the mathematical source of ill-posedness:
similar option prices can correspond to completely different volatility surfaces,
so the inversion of $F$ is unstable.

\subsubsection{Tikhonov functional}

Let $u^\delta$ denote the (noisy) observed option prices with $\|u^\delta - u^\dagger\|_{L_2(Q)} \le \delta$,
where $u^\dagger = u(a^\dagger)$ for the true (unknown) parameter $a^\dagger$.
Following \cite{egger2005tikhonov} we stabilize the inverse problem by minimizing the
\emph{Tikhonov functional}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tikhonov_functional}
    J^\beta(a) = \|u(a) - u^\delta\|^2_{L_2} + \beta\,\|a - a^*\|^2_{H^1},
\end{equation}
where $\beta > 0$ is the regularization parameter, $u^\delta$ is the observation
(option prices at a fixed maturity $T_0$), and $a^*$ is the \emph{a~priori} guess that
encodes any structural knowledge about the volatility (e.g., smoothness or decay for large $|y|$).
The first term penalizes mismatch with data; the second term penalizes deviation of $a$
from $a^*$ in $H^1$, which promotes smooth reconstructions.

The $H^1$ norm in the penalty is
\begin{equation*}
    \|a - a^*\|^2_{H^1} = \int_{-M}^{M} \bigl[(a-a^*)^2 + (\partial_y(a-a^*))^2\bigr]\,dy.
\end{equation*}

\begin{proposition}[\cite{egger2005tikhonov}, Proposition 3.1--3.2]
For any $\beta > 0$ a minimizer $a^\delta_\beta$ of $J^\beta$ exists.
If $\delta \to 0$ and $\beta(\delta) \to 0$ with $\delta^2/\beta(\delta) \to 0$,
then every sequence of minimizers $\{a^{\delta_k}_{\beta(\delta_k)}\}$
has a convergent subsequence, and every limit point is an $a^*$-minimum-norm solution.
\end{proposition}

\subsubsection{Gradient computation via the adjoint method}

To minimize $J^\beta$ by gradient-based methods we need its Gâteaux derivative.
The \emph{directional derivative} of the data-fidelity term at $a$ in direction $h$ is
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\|u(a+\varepsilon h) - u^\delta\|^2_{L_2}\Big|_{\varepsilon=0}
    = 2\bigl(u(a) - u^\delta,\; u'(a)[h]\bigr)_{L_2},
\end{equation*}
where $u'(a)[h] = w(\cdot, T_0)$ is the solution of the \emph{linearized} forward equation,
obtained by differentiating~\eqref{eq:inverse_pde_1} in the direction $h$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gateaux_derivative}
    -w_\tau + a(w_{yy} - w_y) - (r-q)\,w_y - q\,w = -h\,(u_{yy} - u_y),
    \quad w(y,0)=0, \quad w\big|_{\partial\Omega}=0.
\end{equation}
Rather than solving~\eqref{eq:gateaux_derivative} for every direction $h$,
the gradient is evaluated efficiently via a single \emph{adjoint solve}.
Let $V(y,\tau)$ solve the backward parabolic problem
\begin{equation}\label{eq:adjoint_pde}
    V_\tau + (aV)_{yy} + (aV)_y + (r-q)V_y - qV = 0,
    \qquad V(y, T_0) = u(a)(y, T_0) - u^\delta(y),
\end{equation}
with homogeneous Dirichlet conditions at $y = \pm M$ and integrated backward in $\tau$.
Then the Gâteaux derivative satisfies \cite{egger2005tikhonov}
\begin{equation*}
    \bigl(u'(a)[h],\, u(a)-u^\delta\bigr)_{L_2}
    = \int_0^{T_0}\!\!\int_{-M}^{M} h(y)\,(u_{yy}-u_y)\,V\,dy\,d\tau.
\end{equation*}
Adding the $H^1$ regularization term, the total gradient direction (in $L^2$ sense) is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tikhonov_gradient}
    \langle \nabla J^\beta(a),\, h \rangle
    = 2\int_0^{T_0}\!\!\int_{-M}^{M} h\,(u_{yy}-u_y)\,V\,dy\,d\tau
      + 2\beta\int_{-M}^{M}\bigl[(a-a^*)h + \partial_y(a-a^*)\partial_y h\bigr]dy.
\end{equation}
In the finite-element implementation the data term is assembled in the equivalent form
obtained by integrating $(u_{yy}-u_y)$ by parts against the basis functions,
which avoids a mass-matrix solve and yields the Euclidean gradient directly.
The second term is the $H^1$ inner product $(a-a^*, h)_{H^1}$,
whose Euclidean (DOF-level) representation is $(M + K)(a - a^*)$,
where $M$ and $K$ are the finite-element mass and stiffness matrices.

\subsubsection{Choice of $\beta$ and stopping criterion}

In the numerical experiments of \cite{egger2005tikhonov} the regularization parameter
is chosen by a decreasing sequence
\begin{equation*}
    \beta_0 = 10^{2}\delta > \beta_1 > \cdots > \beta_n = 10^{-3}\delta,
\end{equation*}
minimizing $J^{\beta_i}$ sequentially, warm-starting each minimization from the previous minimizer.
The sequence is stopped when the Morozov discrepancy principle
$\|u(a^\delta_\beta) - u^\delta\| \le \tau\delta$ is satisfied for some $\tau > 1$;
across the noise levels $\delta \in [10^{-5}, 10^{-1}]$ tested in \cite{egger2005tikhonov},
the choice $\tau = 1.5$ is met at typical values $\beta \sim 10^{-2}\delta$.
For exact data ($\delta = 0$) a single fixed $\beta$ is used.

\subsubsection{Variational forms and finite-element discretization}

The forward PDE~\eqref{eq:inverse_pde_1} is discretized in space with
$P_1$ finite elements on a uniform mesh of $[-M, M]$ and in time with the
implicit Euler scheme.
Multiplying by a test function $v \in H^1_0(-M,M)$ and integrating by parts gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:variational_forward_eq}
    \frac{d}{d\tau}\int_{-M}^{M} u\,v\,dy
    = -\int_{-M}^{M} a\,u_y\,v_y\,dy
      -\int_{-M}^{M}\partial_y a\,u_y\,v\,dy
      -\int_{-M}^{M}(a + r - q)\,u_y\,v\,dy
      -\int_{-M}^{M} q\,u\,v\,dy.
\end{equation}
The adjoint equation~\eqref{eq:adjoint_pde} is run backward in $\tau$;
its weak form (with $m = V$, $m_\tau = -(am)_{yy} - (am)_y - (r-q)m_y + qm$) is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:variational_adjoint_eq}
    \frac{d}{d\tau}\int_{-M}^{M} m\,v\,dy
    = \int_{-M}^{M}(am)_y\,v_y\,dy
      +\int_{-M}^{M} a\,m\,v_y\,dy
      +\int_{-M}^{M}(r-q)\,m\,v_y\,dy
      +\int_{-M}^{M} q\,m\,v\,dy.
\end{equation}
Both systems are solved with a direct LU factorization at each time step.
Gradient updates are assembled from~\eqref{eq:tikhonov_gradient} using the stored forward trajectory
and the backward adjoint solution.

\subsubsection{Numerical experiment --- Example 1}

We reproduce Example 1 (case A) of \cite{egger2005tikhonov}:
constant volatility recovery on $y \in [-3,3]$ ($M = 3$), $\tau \in [0,1]$,
with $r = q = 0$ and spot $S_0 = 1000$.%
\footnote{The paper's text states $S_0 = 100$, but its tabulated option values
(e.g.\ a call worth $439$ at $K = 600$) and test~(D) (``an underlying worth $1000\$$'')
are only consistent with $S_0 = 1000$; we use the latter. The recovered $a(y)$ is
scale-invariant, but the Tikhonov balance is not---the data term scales as $S_0^2$
while the penalty does not---so reproducing the paper's $\beta$ requires its $S_0$.}
The true parameter is
\[
    a^\dagger = 0.15 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \sigma^\dagger = \sqrt{2 \times 0.15} \approx 0.548.
\]
Data $u^\delta$ are the exact option prices at $\tau = T = 1$ generated from the
Black--Scholes formula (no noise, case A of the paper).
The initial guess and $a^*$ are both set to
\[
    a^*_1(y) = 0.15 - 0.05\,\operatorname{erf}(-y^2),
\]
which agrees with the true value at the money and flattens out ($\partial_y a^* \to 0$,
so $a^* \to 0.20$) for large $|y|$---the asymptotic behaviour invoked in
\cite{egger2005tikhonov} for the convergence-rate theorems 4.1--4.2.
The regularization parameter is fixed at $\beta = 10^{-6}$ (exact data, so a small noise level).

The PDE is discretized with $199$ linear ($P_1$) elements on $[-M, M]$---clustered
toward the money by a $\sinh$ node map, since the price data are informative only near
$y = 0$---and $200$ implicit-Euler steps in $\tau$, and the Tikhonov functional is
minimized by L-BFGS-B using the adjoint gradient~\eqref{eq:tikhonov_gradient}.
Figure~\ref{fig:egger_ex1} shows the result: the recovered $a(y)$ matches the true
constant $a^\dagger = 0.15$ across the strike range the data constrain (reproducing
Tables~1--2 of \cite{egger2005tikhonov} to four digits), while in the deep
in-/out-of-the-money tails---where the prices carry almost no volatility
information---it reverts to the \emph{a~priori} guess $a^*_1$, a direct manifestation
of the ill-posedness discussed above.

\subsubsection{Alternative approach: Interpolation/Extrapolation of the IV surface}\label{sec:impl_vol}

The Tikhonov approach above inverts the forward pricing operator directly and is robust to
the noise and sparsity of market quotes, at the cost of solving a regularized optimization
problem. A computationally lighter alternative is to first construct a smooth,
arbitrage-free \emph{implied volatility surface} from the quoted options---by interpolating
in the liquid region and extrapolating into the wings---convert it to a continuous call-price
surface $C(K,T)$ through the Black--Scholes formula, and then read off the local volatility
by applying the Dupire formula~\eqref{eq:dupire_formula} pointwise. Here the regularization is
implicit, supplied by the smoothness of the chosen interpolant rather than by an explicit
penalty, and the no-arbitrage conditions of the previous section must be enforced on the
fitted surface for the resulting local variance to stay positive.

We build such a surface from market data and use it as the input to the Dupire formula;
the full construction---risk-free curve, dividend-yield calibration, and the out-of-the-money
composite from which the smiles are assembled---is detailed in
Appendix~\ref{app:impl_vol}.

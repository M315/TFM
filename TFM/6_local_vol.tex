\section{Local Volatility Model}

We are going to introduce the Local Volatility Model mainly following \cite{bergomi_stochastic_vol}.

\subsection{Model description}

The \textbf{local volatility model} is the simplest and most widely used volatility model in derivatives pricing.
It extends the Black-Scholes framework by allowing the instantaneous volatility to be a deterministic function of both time and the spot price of the underlying asset,
\begin{equation*}
    \sigma = \sigma(t, S).
\end{equation*}
So the dynamics of the underlying asset under the local volatility model are given by the following stochastic differential equation,
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{dS_t}{S_t} = (r - q)\, dt + \sigma(t, S_t)\, dW_t,
\end{equation*}
and the pricing PDE is identical to the Black-Scholes PDE,
with the deterministic volatility function replacing the constant volatility
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} =
        r f(t, S_t)
        - (r - q) S_t \frac{\partial f}{\partial S}
        - \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2(t, S_t) S_t^2 \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial S^2},
\end{equation*}

\subsubsection{Market model}

A market model is a valuation model that includes vanilla options as part of the underlying,
this means we are assuming that when hedging a derivative,
we can use the vanilla options as a hedging instrument,
in other words,
the derivative can be replicated using a combination of the underlying as well as vanilla options.
Thus vanilla options under a market model are exactly priced.

The local volatility model is a market model,
since it is calibrated to match the vanilla option prices observed in the market

\subsection{Dupire Equation}

The Dupire equation, introduced in \cite{dupire_pricing_smile},
expresses the local volatility function $\sigma(t, S)$ directly in terms of derivatives
of call option prices with respect to strike and maturity.
We present the derivation following \cite{bergomi_stochastic_vol}.

\subsubsection{Derivation}

Consider the following general diffusive dynamics for $S_t$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:log-normal_diffusion}
    dS_t = (r - q)\,S_t\,dt + \sigma_t\,S_t\,dW_t,
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_t$ is an arbitrary adapted process.
The price of a European call option with strike $K$ and maturity $T$ under the risk-neutral measure is:
\begin{equation*}
    C(K, T) = e^{-rT}\,\E\!\left[(S_T - K)^+\right].
\end{equation*}

Before applying It\^{o}'s formula we recall the two generalized functions
that appear as distributional derivatives of $(x)^+ = \max(x, 0)$.

\begin{definition}[Heaviside function]
The \textbf{Heaviside function} $\theta : \R \to \{0,1\}$ is defined by
\begin{equation*}
    \theta(x) =
    \begin{cases}
        1 & x > 0, \\
        0 & x < 0.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation*}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Dirac delta]
The \textbf{Dirac delta} $\delta$ is the distributional derivative of $\theta$,
characterized by the property that for any smooth test function $\varphi$:
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} \varphi(x)\,\delta(x)\,dx = \varphi(0).
\end{equation*}
\end{definition}

These two distributions arise as the successive generalized derivatives of $(x)^+$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:heaviside_delta_max}
    \frac{d}{dx}(x)^+ = \theta(x),
    \qquad
    \frac{d^2}{dx^2}(x)^+ = \delta(x).
\end{equation}
The first identity follows from $(x)^+ = x\,\theta(x)$ and $x\,\delta(x) = 0$;
the second is the distributional derivative of $\theta$.

To relate option prices to the dynamics of $S_t$, we apply It\^{o}'s formula
to $(S_T - K)^+$ over the interval $[T, T + dT]$, using \eqref{eq:heaviside_delta_max}
with $x = S_T - K$:
\begin{align*}
    d(S_T - K)^+
    &= \theta(S_T - K)\bigl((r-q)\,S_T\,dT + \sigma_T\,S_T\,dW_T\bigr) \\
    &\quad + \frac{1}{2}\,\delta(S_T - K)\,\sigma_T^2\,S_T^2\,dT.
\end{align*}
We work with undiscounted prices $\mathcal{C}(K,T) = e^{rT} C(K,T)$.
Differentiating the identity $\mathcal{C}(K,T) = \E[(S_T - K)^+]$ with respect to $K$ gives:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dupire_density}
    \E\!\left[\theta(S_T - K)\right] = -\frac{\partial \mathcal{C}}{\partial K},
    \qquad
    \E\!\left[\delta(S_T - K)\right] = \frac{\partial^2 \mathcal{C}}{\partial K^2}.
\end{equation}
The second identity is the Breeden--Litzenberger result \cite{breeden_litzenberger}: the second strike derivative
of undiscounted call prices equals the risk-neutral density of $S_T$.
To see why, let $p(\cdot, T)$ denote the risk-neutral density of $S_T$, so that
\begin{equation*}
    \mathcal{C}(K, T)
        = \E[(S_T - K)^+]
        = \int_0^{\infty} (s - K)^+\, p(s, T)\,ds
        = \int_K^{\infty} (s - K)\, p(s, T)\,ds.
\end{equation*}
Differentiating with respect to $K$ (using Leibniz's rule, noting the lower limit contributes zero
because $(K-K)^+ = 0$):
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\partial \mathcal{C}}{\partial K}
        = -\int_K^{\infty} p(s, T)\,ds
        = -(1 - F(K, T)),
\end{equation*}
where $F(K, T) = P(S_T \leq K)$ is the risk-neutral CDF.
A second differentiation gives:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:breeden_litzenberger}
    \frac{\partial^2 \mathcal{C}}{\partial K^2} = p(K, T).
\end{equation}
Since $\E[\delta(S_T - K)] = \int_0^\infty \delta(s - K)\,p(s,T)\,ds = p(K,T)$,
this is exactly the second identity in~\eqref{eq:dupire_density}.
In particular, the positivity of $p$ guarantees $\partial^2 \mathcal{C}/\partial K^2 \geq 0$,
which we will use when discussing no-arbitrage conditions.

From the identity $\mathcal{C} = \E[S_T\,\theta(S_T - K)] - K\,\E[\theta(S_T - K)]$ we also obtain:
\begin{equation*}
    \E\!\left[S_T\,\theta(S_T - K)\right] = \mathcal{C} - K\frac{\partial \mathcal{C}}{\partial K}.
\end{equation*}

Taking the expectation of the It\^{o} expansion (noting $\E[dW_T] = 0$) and substituting these identities:
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
    \frac{\partial \mathcal{C}}{\partial T}\,dT
        & = (r-q)\,\E[S_T\,\theta(S_T-K)]\,dT
        + \frac{1}{2}\,\E\!\left[\sigma_T^2\,S_T^2\,\delta(S_T - K)\right]dT \\
        & = (r-q)\!\left(\mathcal{C} - K\frac{\partial \mathcal{C}}{\partial K}\right)dT
        + \frac{1}{2}\,\E\!\left[\sigma_T^2\,S_T^2\,\delta(S_T - K)\right]dT.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
For the remaining term, we use the sifting property of the Dirac delta:
since $\delta(S_T - K)$ is supported at $S_T = K$, the factor $S_T^2$ can be replaced by $K^2$ inside any expectation,
\begin{equation*}
    \E\!\left[\sigma_T^2\,S_T^2\,\delta(S_T - K)\right] = K^2\,\E\!\left[\sigma_T^2\,\delta(S_T - K)\right],
\end{equation*}
giving:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\partial \mathcal{C}}{\partial T}\,dT
    = (r-q)\!\left(\mathcal{C} - K\frac{\partial \mathcal{C}}{\partial K}\right)dT
    + \frac{K^2}{2}\,\E\!\left[\sigma_T^2\,\delta(S_T - K)\right]dT.
\end{equation*}
Solving for $\E[\sigma_T^2\,\delta(S_T - K)]$, dividing by
$\E[\delta(S_T - K)] = \partial^2\mathcal{C}/\partial K^2$,
and reverting to discounted prices $C = e^{-rT}\mathcal{C}$, we obtain:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dupire_general}
    \E\!\left[\sigma_T^2 \,\middle|\, S_T = K\right]
    = 2\,\frac{\dfrac{\partial C}{\partial T} + qC + (r-q)\,K\,\dfrac{\partial C}{\partial K}}
           {K^2\,\dfrac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}}.
\end{equation}
This identity holds for any model of the lognormal-type form \ref{eq:log-normal_diffusion},
with $\sigma_t$ an arbitrary adapted process \cite{bergomi_stochastic_vol}.
The multiplicative structure $\sigma_t S_t$ is essential: it is what produces the factor $S_T^2$ in the It\^{o} expansion,
which the sifting property then converts to $K^2$.
A consequence is that the smile alone does not determine $\sigma_t$:
two processes $\sigma_t$ and $\sigma_t'$ generate the same vanilla smile if and only if
$\E[\sigma_T^2 \mid S_T = K] = \E[\sigma_T^{\prime 2} \mid S_T = K]$ for all $K$ and $T$.

In the local volatility model, where $\sigma_t \equiv \sigma(t, S_t)$,
the conditional expectation reduces to $\sigma(t,S)^2$,
and evaluating at $K = S$, $T = t$ gives the \textbf{Dupire formula}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dupire_formula}
    \sigma(t, S)^2 = 2\,\frac{
        \dfrac{\partial C}{\partial T} + qC + (r-q)\,K\,\dfrac{\partial C}{\partial K}
    }{
        K^2\,\dfrac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}
    }\Bigg|_{\substack{K = S,\\ T = t}}.
\end{equation}
Given the full surface of observed call prices $C(K,T)$,
equation~\eqref{eq:dupire_formula} uniquely determines the local volatility function.

\subsubsection{Forward equation}

Rearranging \eqref{eq:dupire_general} for a known local volatility function $\sigma(t, S)$
yields the \textbf{Dupire forward equation}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dupire_forward}
    \frac{\partial C}{\partial T} + (r-q)\,K\,\frac{\partial C}{\partial K}
    - \frac{\sigma^2(T,K)}{2}\,K^2\,\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2} = -qC.
\end{equation}
Unlike the Black-Scholes backward PDE, which is solved backwards in $t$
from a terminal payoff for a single strike and maturity,
the forward equation propagates forward in $T$ with initial condition
$C(K, T = 0) = (S_0 - K)^+$,
producing prices for a fixed initial spot $S_0$ but all strikes $K$ and maturities $T$ simultaneously.

\subsubsection{No-arbitrage conditions}

For the Dupire formula~\eqref{eq:dupire_formula} to yield a positive local variance,
both the numerator and denominator of~\eqref{eq:dupire_general} must be strictly positive,
which is equivalent to requiring the option surface to be free of arbitrage.

\paragraph{Strike arbitrage.}
From~\eqref{eq:dupire_density}, the denominator satisfies:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\partial^2 C(K,T)}{\partial K^2} = e^{-rT}\,\E\!\left[\delta(S_T - K)\right] \geq 0,
\end{equation*}
since it is proportional to the risk-neutral density of $S_T$.
A violation can be exploited via a \textit{butterfly spread}:
buying $\varepsilon^{-2}$ calls at strike $K-\varepsilon$,
buying $\varepsilon^{-2}$ calls at strike $K+\varepsilon$,
and selling $2\varepsilon^{-2}$ calls at strike $K$.
This portfolio has a non-negative payoff at maturity for any realisation of $S_T$,
so its inception value must be non-negative, implying $\partial^2 C/\partial K^2 \geq 0$.

\paragraph{Maturity arbitrage.}
The numerator of~\eqref{eq:dupire_general} can be rewritten as:
\begin{equation*}
    e^{-qT}\frac{d}{dT}\!\left[e^{qT}\,C\!\left(K e^{(r-q)T}, T\right)\right],
\end{equation*}
which is non-negative if and only if $e^{qT}$ times the price of a call struck at
a fixed proportion of the forward $F_T = S_0 e^{(r-q)T}$ is non-decreasing in maturity.
A violation constitutes a \textit{maturity arbitrage}:
one can buy a longer-dated option and sell a shorter-dated one at a net positive premium,
with a delta position that generates non-negative P\&L at both maturities.

In an arbitrage-free market both conditions hold and
the Dupire local volatility function is well-defined.

\subsection{Calibration problem}

In both the Black-Scholes model and the Local Volatility model,
the parameter must be calibrated to market data to make the model usable.
We will focus on the calibration of the Volatility function $\sigma(S, t)$ in the Local Volatility model,
and will assume that the risk-free rate $r$ and the dividend yield $q$ are known and constant.

The market data consists of option prices for different strikes $K$ and maturities $T$,
So the calibration problem is to find a function $\sigma(S, t)$ such that the model prices $C(S_0, t_0, T, K; \sigma)$ match the observed in the market.
While the volatility function could be any function,
usually it is assumed to be smooth and have some regularity.

\subsection{Calibration of the Local Volatility Model - Inverse PDE problem}

We will present a calibration method based on the inverse problem following \cite{inverse_option_calibration}.

\begin{definition}[Inverse Problem of Option Pricing (IPOP)]
\label{def:ipop}
Given market call prices $C^*(K, T)$ observed for a finite set of strikes $K$ and maturities $T$,
find the local volatility function $\sigma = \sigma(S, t)$,
or equivalently the diffusion coefficient $a(y, \tau) = \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2$,
such that the solution of the Dupire forward problem \eqref{eq:inverse_pde_1}--\eqref{eq:inverse_pde_3} satisfies
\begin{equation*}
    u_a(y, \tau_i) = g_i(y) \qquad \text{for each observed maturity } \tau_i,
\end{equation*}
where $g_i(y)$ is the interpolated market price surface at maturity $\tau_i$ expressed in log-moneyness coordinates.
\end{definition}

The IPOP is inherently ill-posed in the sense of Hadamard for three reasons.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Underdeterminacy.}
    Market prices are quoted only for a finite, discrete set of strikes and maturities,
    so the data are insufficient to uniquely determine the function $a(y,\tau)$ over a continuum.
    \item \textbf{Noisy data.}
    Quoted prices are affected by bid-ask spreads and market microstructure noise,
    so the observed prices $g^\varepsilon$ satisfy $\|g^\varepsilon - g\| \leq \varepsilon$ for some noise level $\varepsilon > 0$ relative to an ideal noise-free surface $g$.
    \item \textbf{Instability.}
    The generalized inverse of the propagation operator $A_f$ is typically unbounded:
    small perturbations in price data can induce large oscillations in the reconstructed volatility surface.
\end{itemize}
These properties motivate the use of regularisation techniques to obtain a stable, smooth reconstruction.

\subsubsection{Change of variable - Log-moneyness}

Market option prices are quoted over a discrete set of strikes $K$ and maturities $T$,
but the absolute level of the strike depends on the current price of the underlying asset $S_0$.
To avoid this dependency and make all options comparable,
we will work with what is called the \textit{log-moneyness},
defined as
\begin{equation*}
    y = \log(K/S_0),
\end{equation*}
the \textit{log-moneyness} indicates how far in or out of the money is an option in logarithmic scale.

We redefine the notation of the volatility function and the call option price to reassemble a more standard for PDEs,
let us define $\tau = T$ the time to maturity,
$a(y, \tau) = \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2(K, T)$ as the diffusion parameter,
and $u(\tau, y) = C(S_0, t_0; T, K)$ the call option price,
to do this change of variable to the Dupire equation first we compute the derivatives of $C$ in terms of $u$,
with respect to $T$,
\begin{equation*}
    C_T 
        = \frac{\partial u}{\partial \tau} 
        = u_{\tau},
\end{equation*}
with respect to $K$ we have to apply the chain rule,
\begin{equation*}
    C_K
        = \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} \frac{\partial y}{\partial K}
        = u_y \frac{1}{K},
\end{equation*}
and,
\begin{equation*}
    C_{KK}
        = \frac{\partial}{\partial K} \frac{u_y}{K}
        = \frac{1}{K} \frac{\partial u_y}{\partial K} - \frac{u_y}{K^2}
        = \frac{1}{K^2} (u_{yy} - u_y),
\end{equation*}
thus the Dupire PDE becomes,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inverse_pde_1}
    u_\tau = a(y, \tau) (u_{yy} - u_y) - (r - q) u_y - q u.
\end{equation}
We consider the PDE on a bounded domain $\Omega = (\alpha, \beta) \times (0, T]$,
with Dirichlet boundary conditions,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inverse_pde_2}
    u(\tau, \alpha) = \Psi_1(\tau), \quad
    u(\tau, \beta) = \Psi_2(\tau),
\end{equation}
and initial condition,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inverse_pde_3}
    u(0, y) = f(y) = S_0 \max(1 - e^y, 0).
\end{equation}

Thus the inverse problem is to identify the diffusion parameter $a(y, \tau)$ from the initial value boundary problem defined by $(\ref{eq:inverse_pde_1})$, $(\ref{eq:inverse_pde_2})$, $(\ref{eq:inverse_pde_3})$.

\subsubsection{Reconstruction algorithm}

The proposed algorithm on \cite{inverse_option_calibration} considers to solve the problem on periods of maturities $[T_i, T_{i + 1}]$,
for each maturity with option price data.

The idea is to consider the option price function $f(y)$ at the beginning of the period $T_i$ and $g(y)$ at the end of the period $T_{i + 1}$,
then we consider a linear operator $A_f$ that maps the diffusion parameter $a$ to the price function at the end of the period,
\begin{equation*}
    A_f(a) = u_a(T_{i + 1}, y) = g(y),
\end{equation*}
and we want to find $a$ such that this equality holds.

This approach transforms the nonlinear inverse problem into a series of linear problems,
that are solvable using a Newton-type method.

\subsubsection{Linear operator - Gateaux derivative}

To apply Newton-like methods we first need to compute the Gâteaux derivative of the operator $A_f$.
This will give a linear approximation of the operator around a given point,
that we will use to transform our nonlinear problem into a series of linear problems.

\begin{proposition}
Let $a \in L^\infty(\Omega)$ be a coefficient such that the forward problem admits a unique solution $u_a$.
The Gâteaux derivative of $A_f$ at $a$ in the direction $h$,
denoted by $A_f'(a)$, is given by $A_f'(a)h = w(\cdot, T)$,
where $w(y, \tau)$ is the unique solution to the linearized variational problem:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gateaux_derivative}
    - w_\tau + a(y, \tau)(w_{yy} - w_y) - (r-q)w_y - qw
    = - h(y, \tau)( (u_a)_{yy} - (u_a)_y ),
\end{equation}
subject to homogeneous initial and boundary conditions:
\begin{align}
    w(0, y) &= 0, & y \in (\alpha, \beta), \\
    w(\tau, \alpha) &= 0, \quad w(\tau, \beta) = 0, & \tau \in [0, T].
\end{align}
\end{proposition}


\subsubsection{Adjoint operator}

To implement gradient-based minimization algorithms efficiently,
we introduce the adjoint of the Gâteaux derivative,
referred as the backpropagation operator in \cite{inverse_option_calibration}.

Let $A_f'(a)^*: L^2(\Omega) \to L^2(\Omega \times [0, T])$ denote the adjoint operator.
For a given residual $\psi \in L^2(\Omega)$ (e.g., $\psi = A_f(a) - g$),
the action of the adjoint operator is defined via the solution to a backward parabolic problem.

\begin{proposition}[Adjoint Operator]
Let $u_a$ be the solution to the forward problem for a given volatility parameter $a$. For any $\psi \in L^2(\Omega)$, let $m(y, \tau)$ be the solution to the following adjoint backward parabolic equation:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:adjoint_pde}
    \frac{\partial m}{\partial \tau}
    + \frac{\partial^2 a(y, \tau) m}{\partial y^2}
    + \frac{\partial a(y, \tau) m}{\partial y}
    + (r-q)\frac{\partial m}{\partial y}
    - q m
    = 0,
\end{equation}
subject to the final and boundary conditions:
\begin{align}
    m(y, T) &= \psi(y), & y \in (\alpha, \beta), \\
    m(\alpha, \tau) &= 0, \quad m(\beta, \tau) = 0, & \tau \in [0, T).
\end{align}
Then, the adjoint operator applied to $\psi$ is given by:
\begin{equation}
    A_f'(a)^* \psi 
        = m \left(
            \frac{\partial^2 u_a}{\partial y^2} 
            - \frac{\partial u_a}{\partial y} 
            \right).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

In the case where the volatility parameter $a(y)$ is assumed to be time-independent, the adjoint operator maps into $L^2(\alpha, \beta)$ by integrating the time-dependent sensitivity over the duration of the option:
\begin{corollary}[Time-Independent Case]
If $a = a(y)$, the gradient of the objective function with respect to $a$ is given by:
\begin{equation}
    A_f'(a)^* \psi 
        = \int_0^T m(\cdot, \tau) \left(
            \frac{\partial^2 u_a}{\partial y^2}(\cdot, \tau) 
            - \frac{\partial u_a}{\partial y}(\cdot, \tau) 
            \right) \, d\tau.
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}


\subsubsection{Algorithm}

As mentioned before the time dimension is not used in this procedure,
instead what we do is discretize the time from $t$ to $T$ into intervals and forward propagate the function $u$ using $A_f$ for each interval,
then we compute the residual with respect to $g$ at $T$ and use the backpropagation operator to compute the gradients for each interval,
backwards, and use them to update the parameter $a$.
The calibration algorithm proceeds as follow:

\begin{algorithmic}
    \State Set the functional spaces for $a$ and $u$.
    \State Set the boundary and initial conditions for the forward problem.
    \State Initialize the volatility parameter $a$.
    \State Compute the target option price function $g(y)$ from market data.
    \While{$\| u_a(y) - g(y) \| > tolerance$}
        \State $u_a \gets A_f(a)$
        \State $\nabla_{a}^{*} \gets A'_f(a)^{*}(u_a - g)$
        \State $a \gets$ Update $a$ using gradient descent step.
    \EndWhile
\end{algorithmic}

\paragraph{Positivity of the diffusion coefficient.}
Since $a(y, \tau) = \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2(K, T)$ is a variance it must be strictly positive everywhere,
otherwise the forward PDE loses its parabolic character and the solution degenerates.
Any gradient step that would make $a$ negative at some points is corrected by projecting the update onto
the admissible set $D(A_f) = \{ a : a \geq a_{\min} > 0 \}$,
replacing negative or too-small values by the floor $a_{\min}$.

\paragraph{Gradient descent versus Levenberg--Marquardt.}
The reference \cite{inverse_option_calibration} proposes an inexact Newton method of Levenberg-Marquardt (LM) type
combined with Tikhonov regularization of each linearized subproblem,
\begin{equation*}
    \min_{a}\,\bigl\|A_f(a_n) - g^\varepsilon + A_f'(a_n)(a - a_n)\bigr\|^2 + \alpha_n\,\|a - a_n\|^2,
\end{equation*}
with the regularization parameter $\alpha_n$ chosen via the Morozov discrepancy principle,
giving strong stability guarantees for noisy data.
For the present implementation we use the simpler \textit{L-BFGS-B} gradient descent,
which is sufficient to validate the pipeline and avoids the additional complexity of an inner CG loop.
The two approaches will be compared experimentally in future work.

\subsubsection{Practical implementation}

We have implemented the calibration algorithm in \textit{Python} using the \textit{FENICS} library,
so we use finite elements to solve the forward and backward PDEs.

Also we used \textit{scipy} to implement the optimization algorithm,
in particular we used the \textit{L-BFGS-B} algorithm to perform the minimization of the objective function.

First we needed to transform the PDEs into their variational forms.
We multiply equation $(\ref{eq:inverse_pde_1})$ by a test function $v \in H^1_0(\Omega)$ and integrate over $(\alpha, \beta)$,
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} u_\tau\,v\,dy
        = \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} a\,u_{yy}\,v\,dy
        - \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} (a + r - q)\,u_y\,v\,dy
        - \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} q\,u\,v\,dy.
\end{equation*}
For the second-order term we integrate by parts, using $(au_y)_y = a u_{yy} + a_y u_y$,
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} a\,u_{yy}\,v\,dy
        = \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} (a u_y)_y\,v\,dy
        = - \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} a\,u_y\,v_y\,dy
        + \left[ a\,u_y\,v \right]_{\alpha}^{\beta},
\end{equation*}
since $v \in H^1_0(\Omega)$ the boundary term vanishes, $\left[ a\,u_y\,v \right]_{\alpha}^{\beta} = 0$.
Since $v$ does not depend on $\tau$,
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} u_\tau\,v\,dy = \frac{d}{d\tau}\int_{\alpha}^{\beta} u\,v\,dy.
\end{equation*}
Thus the variational form of the forward equation is,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:variational_forward_eq}
    \frac{d}{d \tau} \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} u\,v\,dy
        = - \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} a\,u_y\,v_y\,dy
        - \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} (a + r - q)\,u_y\,v\,dy
        - \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} q\,u\,v\,dy.
\end{equation}

For equation $(\ref{eq:adjoint_pde})$,
we multiply by $v \in H^1_0(\Omega)$ and integrate over $(\alpha, \beta)$.
The PDE reads $m_\tau = -(am)_{yy} - (am)_y - (r-q)m_y + qm$, so
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} m_\tau\,v\,dy
    = - \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} (am)_{yy}\,v\,dy
    - \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} (am)_y\,v\,dy
    - \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} (r-q)\,m_y\,v\,dy
    + \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} q\,m\,v\,dy.
\end{equation*}
Integrating by parts the second-order term,
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} (am)_{yy}\,v\,dy
        = - \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} (am)_y\,v_y\,dy
        + \left[ (am)_y\,v \right]_{\alpha}^{\beta}
        = - \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} (am)_y\,v_y\,dy,
\end{equation*}
so $-\int (am)_{yy}\,v\,dy = +\int (am)_y\,v_y\,dy$.
For the first-order terms,
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} (am)_y\,v\,dy
        = - \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} a\,m\,v_y\,dy
        + \left[ am\,v \right]_{\alpha}^{\beta}
        = - \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} a\,m\,v_y\,dy,
\end{equation*}
so $-\int (am)_y\,v\,dy = +\int a\,m\,v_y\,dy$,
and similarly $-\int (r-q)\,m_y\,v\,dy = +\int (r-q)\,m\,v_y\,dy$.
Thus the variational form of the adjoint equation is,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:variational_adjoint_eq}
    \frac{d}{d\tau}\int_{\alpha}^{\beta} m\,v\,dy
        = \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} (am)_y\,v_y\,dy
        + \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} a\,m\,v_y\,dy
        + \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} (r-q)\,m\,v_y\,dy
        + \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} q\,m\,v\,dy.
\end{equation}
